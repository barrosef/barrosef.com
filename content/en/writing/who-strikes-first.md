<p class="drop">There is a sentence that fits in the palm of a hand, and the whole of libertarianism fits inside it. <em>No one may initiate force against another person, nor against what is theirs.</em> It is called the non-aggression principle. It does not say force is forbidden — it says no one may strike first. Whoever defends himself does not aggress. Whoever starts, does.</p>

It sounds like little. It is everything. Read it again and notice what the sentence does not ask: it does not ask whether the blow was well-meant, whether it was voted on, whether it carries a stamp, whether the majority agreed, whether the money goes to a good cause. It asks one thing only — who started. And on that question, so simple it looks naive, the world splits in two.

## Calm sea

Picture a morning with no drama in it. A man wakes in a house he bought, drinks coffee he paid for, gets into a car he cleared in sixty instalments, drives to a workshop he opened with his own savings. He spends the day trading work for money with people who chose to seek him out. No one was forced into anything. Every coin that came in came in because someone wanted it to. Every coin that went out went out because he wanted it to.

That is peace, in the most honest definition I know: a day on which every exchange was voluntary. There is nothing utopian about it. It is the routine of nearly all your relationships — the baker, the mechanic, the neighbour, the customer. You live almost entirely under the non-aggression principle and never notice, the way you never notice the air.

Almost.

## The letter

Because in the middle of that morning an envelope arrives. Inside, a sheet with a barcode and a date. It is not a proposal. It is not a quote. It is an order with a reference number.

Do what almost nobody does: read the letter to the end — not the paper, the logic. Ask what happens if you say no. First the fine. Then the interest. Then the blacklisted name, the frozen account, the car a tow truck hauls off to rot in a yard under the sun. And if you resist each of those steps with the same calm you use on a pushy salesman — if you simply keep saying no — one day an armed man knocks on your door, authorised to use the gun. He is not there because you attacked anyone. He is there because you refused.

That is the secret the tax bill hides under its barcode: at the end of the chain there is always a blow, and you did not strike first. Strip away the paperwork, the vocabulary, the solemnity, and an old scene remains: someone takes what is yours, and the only novelty is that he wears a badge. [The corner of this site that deals with the subject](/about/libertarianism/) calls it by its name. Here the principle is enough: who initiated force? Not you.

## Calm sea, again

What would it look like, then? Less strange than it seems. It would be the morning we started with, stretched over the rest of the day. The road, you would pay to whoever maintains it — and change supplier when it turned into craters. The hospital, you would pay as you pay the dentist, and be called a client rather than a number. The neighbourhood's security, you and your neighbours would hire, and fire whoever failed to show up. None of this is invention; all of it already exists, today, for those who can afford to pay twice — once to the State and once to whoever actually delivers.

Libertarians have a name for this way of arranging life, in which every relationship is born of a yes: voluntaryism. That is for another text. For now, keep only the image — the same morning, without the envelope.

## The circle

Now breathe. Because the worst part is not the letter. The worst part is where some of what it takes ends up.

There is, in Brazil, a fund. Every election year it is filled with money that came in through the bill with the barcode — the 2026 one was set at nearly five billion reais, more than six counting the fund that keeps the parties going the rest of the time — and shared among thirty parties so that they can campaign. Campaign for what? To hold the offices that decide the size of the next bill.

Stop a second on that mechanism, because it is perfect in its obscenity. What was taken becomes billboard, jingle, leaflet, rally. The billboard asks for your vote. The vote hands over the mandate. The mandate signs the budget. The budget fills the fund. The fund pays for the next billboard. It is an endless belt whose raw material is what left your pocket and whose finished product is the permanence of those who took it. No robber ever had this idea: to have the victim finance the advertising for the next robbery, and call it democracy.

And note the fine detail: you cannot say no to this fund even by voting. It is split among everyone. The party you despise receives a slice of your money to persuade you otherwise. You pay for your opponent's speech. You pay for every opponent's speech, at the same time, and the only choice left is which of them will spend your money best.

## The silence after

Under the principle, the scene is almost embarrassingly simple. Whoever wants a campaign pays for it. The candidate asks, the voter decides whether to give, and a campaign without support dies for the right reason: nobody wanted it. There is nothing radical in that — it is how a bakery works, a newspaper, a church, a club. It is how everything works that cannot arrive at your door with an armed man.

An election financed that way would have fewer posters. It would also have a quality that is missing today: every coin in it would be the signature of someone who chose to put it there. Not a residue of the envelope. A yes.

## The other letter

There is a place where the State drops the subtlety and asks for the thing itself.

At eighteen, every young man in this country receives a summons. Not to pay — to report. To hand over, if called, a year of his time, his body, his will, to an institution he did not choose, in a role he did not ask for, under orders he cannot refuse. Most are dismissed, and the dismissal is presented as benevolence. Notice what that means: the default is possession; freedom is the exception granted.

If tax is the State wanting your money, compulsory military service is the State being honest: it wants you. Not your output — your person. Under the principle there is nothing to discuss. A man who attacked no one receives an order to report, and refusal has a name in the code. Who struck first?

## Open sea

And how would a country where no one can be taken defend itself? With those who want to. Soldiers who chose the uniform, paid by those who value what the uniform protects, in forces that have to be good enough for someone to want to join and someone to want to pay. This is not fiction: the most respected armed forces in the world are volunteer forces, and conscript armies are remembered mostly for the ones who did not come back. A country that can only be defended by people forced to do it may be asking the wrong question about what it is defending.

The morning ends as it began. A man, a house, a car, a workshop, a day of exchanges in which no one was made to do anything. The non-aggression principle does not promise that day — it only says that whoever interrupts it by force is wrong, whatever paper he carries in his hand. It fits in one sentence. The hard part is not understanding it. The hard part is looking at the envelope, at the fund, at the summons, and finding the courage to answer the one question the sentence asks.

Who started?
