*Second episode of **AI Fluency**, a series that walks through the AI Fluency
Framework one competency at a time. [Episode 1](/writing/ai-fluency-mapped/) drew
the whole map; this one walks its first branch.*

---

Picture a Thursday evening with a proposal due on Monday. The client is
important, the material is scattered across emails, spreadsheets and your own
memory of three meetings, and the week has left you with very little energy, so
you open an AI assistant and type something close to "write a proposal for this
client to renew our contract". The answer arrives in seconds and it looks like a
proposal: an executive summary, a list of benefits, a confident closing paragraph.
It is also, on a second reading, a proposal that could have been sent to any
client by any company, with figures that came from nowhere and a promise your
team cannot keep, and you spend a good part of the weekend taking it apart.

The instinct at that point is to blame the prompt and to look for a better way
of asking, which is a real skill and the subject of the next episode. Yet the
trouble in that scene began a step earlier, before a single word was typed, at
the moment you handed over the whole job, including the parts only you could do.
The AI Fluency Framework, developed by Rick Dakan, Joseph Feller and Anthropic,
calls that earlier step **Delegation**: setting goals and
deciding whether, when and how to engage with AI, which in practice means
deciding what work should be done by people, what work should be done by AI and
how to distribute it between them. Of the four competencies on the map it is, in
my reading, the one that usually comes first and the one that is easiest to skip,
because skipping it feels like saving time.

The framework splits Delegation into three skills, and the map adds, under each
one, the questions I find myself asking when I practise it. The skills are the
framework's and the one-line summaries condense its definitions, while the
questions are mine, offered as a way in rather than as part of the official text. Read from left to right, the
branch moves from the work to the tool and then to the split between the two,
which is also the order in which the decisions tend to happen.

## Problem awareness: knowing what you are actually asking for

The framework defines **problem awareness** as clearly understanding your goals
and the nature of the work before involving AI. It sounds obvious until you
notice how easily it slips under pressure, because the goal in the proposal
scene was never "a proposal"; it was a renewal, with a client who had doubts
about last year's delivery, and a document that had to answer those doubts with
numbers only your team had. Once the goal is said that way, the nature of the
work changes too, since a good part of it turns out to be gathering facts and
deciding what to promise, and very little of it is writing sentences.

The questions on the map are a short way of getting there. What does finished
look like, so that you would recognise it if you saw it? Who is it for, and what
is at stake if it goes wrong? And what do I know that the AI cannot, the history,
the context, the unwritten constraints, which no system will guess on its own?
Anthropic's own research suggests this step is far from universal. In the AI
Fluency Index, a study published in February 2026 that classified fluency
behaviours in 9,830 conversations on Claude.ai, the behaviour I read as closest
to problem awareness, clarifying the goal before asking for help, appeared in
about half of them, 51.1%, which means that in the other half the goal was never
put into words before the request, much as it was not on that Thursday evening.

## Platform awareness: knowing the colleague you are briefing

The second skill, **platform awareness**, is understanding the capabilities and
limitations of different AI systems, and none of it requires technical
knowledge. It is closer to what you would want to know about a capable new
colleague before giving them a task: what they are good at, what they cannot
possibly know yet and where they tend to bluff. The framework's own glossary
gives the vocabulary for the limits. A model has a **knowledge cutoff**, a date
after which it has no built-in knowledge of the world. It has a **context
window**, a limit on how much information it can consider at once, which varies
from one model to the next. And it can **hallucinate**, stating something
plausible and confident that is simply wrong.

Each of those limits translates directly into a delegation decision: the AI in
the proposal scene could not see your spreadsheets unless you gave them to it, so
the figures it produced were invented rather than retrieved; it knew nothing
about the three meetings, so the promises it made were generic. Tools also differ
from each other, in what they can read, what they can search, what they can
produce and how carefully they handle what you give them, so the same task can
be a good delegation to one system and a poor one to another. Platform awareness
is simply the habit of asking, before you hand something over, whether this tool
is the right one for this part of the work.

## Task delegation: splitting the work by strengths

The third skill, **task delegation**, is where the first two meet: thoughtfully
distributing work between people and AI so as to use the strengths of each.
Anthropic's lesson on the subject adds a line I find useful, that effective
delegation requires both domain expertise and an understanding of what AI can
do. In my reading, that is why a lawyer delegates legal work better than a
developer would, and why the same lawyer still needs to know the tool before the
split can be a good one.

The figure replays the proposal with the work split into steps, which is very
close to the exercise Anthropic's course gives its learners: take a project with
several steps, break it into tasks and plan which ones you will do yourself and
which ones you will do with AI. In my split, deciding what the client must leave
convinced of stays with you, because it depends on what you know about the
relationship, and so does gathering the figures, because they live in your
records and not in the model. Exploring the structure and the likely objections
is done together, as a conversation. Turning the agreed outline into a first
draft goes to the AI, which is fast and tireless at exactly that. Checking every
number, name and promise comes back to you, and so, finally, does signing and
sending it, since it will carry your name. Nothing in that list is
sophisticated, and that is the point: the same tool, given the fourth step
instead of the whole job, produces something you can actually use.

## Choosing how, and choosing not to

The labels on the figure point to the last decision on the branch. The framework
describes three ways people and AI work together: **automation**, where the AI
carries out a specific task on your specific instructions; **augmentation**,
where you think together in a back-and-forth to which both contribute; and
**agency**, where you set the AI up to work on its own on your behalf. In my
reading, choosing among them is part of the "how" in "whether, when and how",
so it belongs squarely to Delegation, and the same piece of
work can call for a different mode at each step, as it does in the proposal.
The AI Fluency Index found that the most common expression of fluency it observed
was augmentative, treating the AI as a thought partner rather than handing work
over entirely. It also found that only about one conversation in ten, 10.1%,
included the user consulting the AI on the approach before asking it to execute,
which in my reading is perhaps the cheapest delegation question there is: asking the
colleague how they would go about it before telling them to start.

The "whether" in that definition deserves its own moment, because Delegation
includes deciding to keep work. Some parts of a task carry judgement that is
yours to exercise, such as what to promise a client or how to read a contract
clause for a particular case; some carry responsibility that cannot be passed
on; and some, for a student above all, exist precisely so that you learn to do
them, in which case handing them over defeats the purpose of the task. A good
delegation leaves those with you on purpose, not by accident, and it is often
the parts you keep that make the parts you hand over worth having.

## The loop that brings you back

Episode 1 showed Delegation joined to Diligence in one of the framework's two
loops, which covers the strategic and ethical decisions about using AI. In my
reading, the loop closes because owning the result teaches you what to delegate
next time: the weekend spent fixing invented figures is exactly what makes you
keep the figures with you on the following proposal. Delegation is therefore not
a decision made once and filed away but one you revise each time a result comes
back, and the fifth episode, on Diligence, will walk the other half of that loop.

## Back to Thursday evening

Return to the proposal and replay the evening with the branch in mind. Before
opening the assistant, you spend a few minutes saying what the proposal is
really for and who will read it; you remember that the tool cannot see your
spreadsheets or your meetings, so you decide to bring the figures yourself; you
split the work, keep the promises and the numbers, think through the structure
together and leave the first draft to the AI. The prompt you finally type is not
especially clever, yet it lands in a much better place, because the decision
behind it, made before you typed and called Delegation by the framework, was a
good one, and that decision works the same way for a contract, a lesson plan, a
tax question or a piece of code.

What it does not tell you is how to say what you need once you have decided to
ask, and that is the subject of the next episode, on Description: how to describe
the product you want, the process the AI should follow and the way it should
work with you.
